# Draft Paper Insert: Frantzman and New Arab Settlement Formation

Seth Frantzman's dissertation provides the strongest settlement-inventory source located so far for the study of Arab and Muslim village formation in late Ottoman and Mandatory Palestine. Its importance lies in its distinction between movement into new settlements and external immigration. Frantzman concludes that 172-195 new Arab settlements were established and permanently inhabited between 1871 and 1948, of which 146 were recognized by Mandatory authorities as inhabited new settlements in 1945. He calculates that 66,940 settlers lived in these new settlements, with 831,486 dunams classified administratively with the newly settled villages by the Mandate.

This finding is significant, but it cannot be used as a direct measure of immigration into Palestine. Frantzman's conclusion is that the inhabitants of the new settlements were mostly local Arabs from nearby areas who founded daughter villages, moved to work as tenants on estates, or underwent sedentarization. He states that very few Arab settlers came from outside the borders of Mandatory Palestine. The dissertation therefore supports a model of substantial internal migration and settlement fixation, while challenging an interpretation that treats the growth of new Arab rural settlement primarily as external immigration.

The dissertation remains useful for the immigration question because it identifies specific external-origin streams that require separate extraction. Frantzman cites Egyptian migration associated with Muhammad Ali's Syrian campaign as the largest nineteenth-century migration that remained in Palestine, and identifies Egyptian settlement in the low-country zones, including Baysan, Wadi Araba, the Jezreel Valley, the Shephelah, the coastal plain, and the Negev. It also provides leads on Algerian-origin settlements in the Safed/Huleh region, including Ammuqa, Marus, and Tuleil.

The evidentiary implication is that the paper should distinguish three categories: external migration, internal migration, and settlement fixation. Frantzman is most useful for the second and third categories, and for identifying village-level leads for the first.
